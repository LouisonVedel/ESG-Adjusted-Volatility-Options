### 6.3.1 Baseline GARCH(1,1) specification
The baseline volatility engine starts from the Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity model of Bollerslev (1986). Let r_t denote the daily log return, expressed in percentage units, and let epsilon_t = r_t - mu denote the innovation around the conditional mean. The conditional variance is h_t = Var(epsilon_t | F_{t-1}). The implemented GARCH(1,1) recursion is h_t = omega + alpha epsilon_(t-1)^2 + beta h_(t-1). Here omega is the long-run variance intercept, alpha is the ARCH or news coefficient measuring the immediate response to a new squared shock, and beta is the GARCH persistence coefficient governing the carry-over of previously estimated variance.
The code estimates this model with the arch Python package using a constant conditional mean, Gaussian innovations, p=1, o=0 and q=1. The model is estimated recursively rather than once on the complete sample. This is essential because the thesis evaluates genuine out-of-sample information rather than a full-sample fitted variance. The one-day-ahead variance is subsequently converted to annualised volatility as sigma_(t+1)^ann = sqrt(h_hat_(t+1) x 252) / 100 because the return input is expressed in percentage points.
It is important to distinguish the parameters of the GARCH recursion from the ESG coefficients introduced later. In the conventional model, alpha and beta belong to the internal conditional-variance dynamics. They are not ESG coefficients. A higher alpha means that the model reacts more strongly to the most recent squared innovation, whereas a higher beta means that the effect of previously estimated variance decays more slowly.
### 6.3.2 Asymmetric GJR-GARCH(1,1) specification
The second baseline family is the Glosten, Jagannathan and Runkle (1993) threshold model, commonly referred to as GJR-GARCH. Its purpose is to capture the asymmetry of equity volatility, whereby negative return shocks can have a different effect on future variance from positive shocks of the same magnitude. The implemented recursion is h_t = omega + alpha epsilon_(t-1)^2 + gamma I(epsilon_(t-1)<0) epsilon_(t-1)^2 + beta h_(t-1). The additional coefficient gamma is therefore the leverage or asymmetry parameter.
In the implementation, GJR-GARCH is generated with arch_model using vol='GARCH', p=1, o=1 and q=1. The o=1 term activates the asymmetric threshold component. The resulting model contains the conventional ARCH coefficient alpha, the asymmetric coefficient gamma, and the persistence coefficient beta. GJR-GARCH therefore does not mean that gamma is the ESG effect: gamma measures the asymmetric response of the baseline volatility process to negative return innovations.
### 6.3.3 EGARCH(1,1) specification
The third family is the Exponential GARCH model of Nelson (1991). EGARCH models the logarithm of conditional variance rather than variance itself. The implemented recursion can be written ln(h_t) = omega + beta ln(h_(t-1)) + alpha(|z_(t-1)| - E|z|) + gamma z_(t-1), where z_(t-1) = epsilon_(t-1)/sqrt(h_(t-1)). Under the Gaussian innovation assumption, E|z| = sqrt(2/pi). Alpha captures the response to shock magnitude, while gamma captures the sign effect.
The code estimates several EGARCH candidate solutions from different starting values and retains only converged and admissible solutions, selecting the candidate with the highest finite log-likelihood. This multi-start procedure reduces sensitivity to pathological numerical solutions. The one-step forecast is then reconstructed directly from the fitted parameters and the last standardised residual before exponentiation. The final ESG stage applies the documented log-variance bounds of -10 and +5.
### 6.3.4 How the ESG augmentation is actually constructed
A central methodological point is that the implemented ESG specifications are not GARCH models in which ESG has simply been inserted as another ARCH or leverage parameter. The code estimates the conventional GARCH, GJR-GARCH and EGARCH dynamics first and then constructs an ESG-augmented forecast through a second-stage regression on the fitted log conditional variance. This is best described as an exogenous-information augmentation or two-stage conditional-variance forecasting architecture.
For each fitted volatility family j, the engine obtains the historical fitted conditional variance h_hat_t^(j), constructs y_t^(j) = ln(h_hat_t^(j)), and constructs the lagged variance state x_t^(j) = ln(h_hat_(t-1)^(j)). The composite ESG relation is y_t^(j) = c_j + phi_j x_t^(j) + delta_E,j E_t + delta_S,j S_t + delta_G,j G_t + u_t. The pillar-specific versions retain only one ESG variable. The ESG variables are standardised within the current training window rather than using future-sample moments.
The 15-model design follows directly from this architecture: three conventional baselines, three composite-ESG specifications, and nine pillar-specific specifications. Environmental, Social and Governance remain distinct information channels. In particular, the Social pillar is not averaged with the Environmental and Governance pillars. This matters because the purpose of the pillar specifications is to determine whether incremental information differs by ESG dimension.
### 6.3.5 Are the ESG coefficients alpha, beta, or gamma?
The answer is no. The ESG coefficients are neither the alpha nor the gamma parameters of the underlying GARCH-family model. In GARCH, alpha is the immediate response of variance to a squared return innovation. In GJR-GARCH, gamma is the incremental response associated with a negative innovation. In EGARCH, alpha controls shock magnitude and gamma controls the sign response in the log-variance equation. The ESG coefficients instead measure the marginal association between standardised ESG information and the fitted log conditional variance, conditional on the lagged fitted variance.
For example, delta_E,j answers a question of the form: holding the lagged fitted variance state constant, how does a one-standard-deviation change in Environmental information relate to the fitted log variance generated by model family j? It should therefore not be interpreted as a shock-response coefficient analogous to alpha or as an asymmetric leverage coefficient analogous to gamma.
The terminology is consequently important. The conventional GARCH-family parameters define the internal dynamics of the volatility process, whereas the ESG coefficients are exogenous-information loadings in the augmentation layer. The empirical contribution of ESG is evaluated through the resulting out-of-sample forecast rather than through a claim that ESG changes the structural meaning of alpha, beta or gamma.
### 6.3.6 From the fitted augmentation to the one-day-ahead ESG forecast
At each forecast date, the engine takes the latest fitted baseline variance state and the ESG value available under the lag convention. For a single-pillar model, the forecast equation is ln(h_hat_(t+1)^ESG) = c_hat_j + phi_hat_j ln(h_hat_t) + delta_hat_k,j Z_(k,t), where k denotes E, S or G. For the composite model, ln(h_hat_(t+1)^ESG) = c_hat_j + phi_hat_j ln(h_hat_t) + delta_hat_E,j Z_E,t + delta_hat_S,j Z_S,t + delta_hat_G,j Z_G,t.
The resulting log variance is transformed back as h_hat_(t+1)^ESG = exp(clip(ln(h_hat_(t+1)^ESG), -10, 5)). The upper bound of +5 implies an annualised volatility ceiling of approximately 193.4 percent. These bounds are numerical safeguards rather than economic assumptions about normal equity volatility. The lower bound prevents numerical underflow, while the upper bound limits pathological explosive forecasts.
The ESG timing rule is explicit in the code: an ESG observation dated in calendar year y is mapped to forecasting year y+1. Thus a score observed during 2018 is eligible for forecasts in 2019 rather than being used contemporaneously in 2018. Within each refit window, the latest annual score consistent with this one-year lag is used. This design prevents annual ESG information from being treated as known before the relevant information date.
### 6.3.7 Recursive out-of-sample estimation and refitting schedule
The forecasting design is strictly recursive. A forecast date t is eligible only when at least 252 prior daily return observations are available. The model is estimated using observations strictly preceding the forecast date. No return dated t or later enters the estimation sample used to produce the forecast for t. This establishes the information-set discipline required for a genuine out-of-sample test.
The models are refitted every 21 trading days. Between refits, previously estimated parameters are retained while the one-day-ahead state is updated using the latest information. This balances computational feasibility and parameter stability: daily refitting can make estimates sensitive to very short-lived changes, whereas very infrequent refitting can allow outdated parameters to dominate changing volatility regimes.
At refit date tau, the process can be summarised as: estimate the baseline family on observations strictly before tau; derive fitted conditional variances; estimate the ESG augmentation on the same training information; standardise ESG using training-window moments; generate daily forecasts until the next refit; then recompute all parameters and standardisation moments at the next refit. The sequence therefore prevents future observations from influencing earlier forecasts.
### 6.3.8 Numerical convergence, failure handling, and auditability
Numerical convergence is treated as part of the empirical methodology rather than hidden inside the results. For conventional GARCH and GJR-GARCH, a non-zero convergence flag triggers a fit failure and the corresponding forecast is not manufactured. For EGARCH, the multi-start procedure attempts several initial parameter configurations. Candidate solutions are retained only when the optimisation converges, the likelihood is finite, and the parameters satisfy the predefined admissibility checks. The candidate with the highest finite log-likelihood is selected.
The final rebuild identified isolated fit failures rather than silently replacing them. Eighty-eight EGARCH failures were recovered through the multi-start correction, while ten GARCH/GJR failures remained represented as missing forecasts. These observations were not imputed, and affected firms were not removed from the broader forecasting panel merely because an isolated date-family combination failed. This preserves the distinction between model availability and firm-level sample eligibility.
The code separates intermediate block outputs, progress information, error logs and the final merged forecast file. Each ticker is processed independently, allowing failed fits or interruptions to be identified at ticker-date-family level. The final forecast file is sorted by ticker, date and model and duplicate ticker-date-model observations are removed at merge time. This makes the empirical output traceable rather than dependent on an opaque single execution.
### 6.3.9 Forecast evaluation and the meaning of the comparison
The forecast target is the next-day realised variance proxy r_(t+1)^2, with returns expressed in percentage units. The primary loss is the squared variance forecast error, L_t = (r_(t+1)^2 - h_hat_(t+1))^2. Because the target and forecast are on the same variance scale, this loss permits direct pairwise comparison across all 15 specifications. Volatility-scale MAE and RMSE are complementary measures after taking the square root of the model variance forecast.
The Diebold-Mariano procedure compares matched forecast-error losses between models. Encompassing regressions provide a different test: they ask whether the ESG forecast contains information that remains useful after conditioning on the corresponding conventional forecast. This distinction is central. An ESG model can have a higher standalone loss and still contain incremental information relative to a benchmark; conversely, a lower average loss does not by itself establish statistical significance or an ESG-specific economic mechanism.
The Model Confidence Set is used as an additional joint comparison of predictive performance. Together, variance-loss measures, Diebold-Mariano tests, encompassing regressions and MCS analysis separate three concepts that should not be conflated: point-estimate forecast accuracy, statistically significant predictive differences, and incremental information conditional on a conventional benchmark.
### 6.3.10 Linking volatility forecasts to Black-76 option valuation
Once the one-day-ahead volatility forecasts are generated, they are carried into the option stage without re-estimating the forecasting models on option information. For a European call under Black-76, C = exp(-rT)[F N(d1) - K N(d2)], while P = exp(-rT)[K N(-d2) - F N(-d1)], with d1 = [ln(F/K) + 0.5 sigma^2 T] / (sigma sqrt(T)) and d2 = d1 - sigma sqrt(T). Here F is the observed LSEG forward price, K is strike, r is the risk-free proxy, T is time to maturity and sigma is the annualised forecast volatility.
The observed call and put are paired at the same firm-date-expiry-strike level to construct the straddle. Forecast volatility therefore enters the theoretical option value through sigma, while market-implied volatility remains an observed market quantity. The empirical comparison is consequently between a physical-measure forecast generated from historical returns and ESG information and the volatility expectation embedded in the option market.
The resulting difference is deliberately called the forecast-implied volatility gap rather than a formal variance risk premium. The option input is observed at a single maturity and strike, while the forecast is a conditional physical-measure expectation. The distinction prevents the empirical pricing deviation from being interpreted as a complete identification of the theoretical risk-neutral variance premium.
### 6.3.11 Trading signal and interpretation of the volatility gap
For each model, the trading signal begins with Gap_t = IV_t - sigma_hat_t. The gap is standardised using a rolling 252-observation standard deviation based only on information available before the trading date: Z_t = Gap_t / SD_252(Gap). A positive Z-score means that market-implied volatility is high relative to the model forecast and generates a short-volatility signal. A negative Z-score means that implied volatility is low relative to the forecast and generates a long-volatility signal. The baseline entry rule is |Z| >= 1.5, with |Z| >= 1.0 and |Z| >= 2.0 used for threshold robustness.
This sign convention is economically intuitive: when implied volatility exceeds the model forecast, the strategy takes the short-volatility side; when implied volatility is below the forecast, it takes the long-volatility side. The signal is model-specific, so replacing the baseline forecast with an ESG-augmented forecast changes both the level of the gap and the standardisation history used to construct Z.
### 6.3.12 Delta and vega hedging mechanics
The trading implementation separates two risk-neutralisation objectives. Delta hedging controls first-order exposure to movements in the underlying equity, whereas vega hedging controls first-order exposure to changes in implied volatility. They are therefore not substitutes. If the target straddle has vega V1 and the longer-maturity hedge straddle has vega V2, the hedge ratio is h = V1 / V2 and the hedge is taken in the opposite direction. The combined position then has approximately zero net vega at entry: V_net = V1 - h V2 approximately equals zero.
The underlying delta is separately neutralised using the observed contract deltas. The vega hedge is selected from observable longer-maturity ATM straddles on the same underlying and date. The algorithm chooses the nearest admissible expiry strictly beyond the target straddle within the predefined 15-to-180-day window and selects the strike closest to the corresponding forward price. No interpolation or synthetic option series is introduced.
If a required observed delta is missing at a hedge date, the corresponding horizon-level trade is excluded rather than imputed. This preserves the principle that every realised P&L observation is generated from an actually observed contract and an explicitly recorded hedge. The final baseline backtest contains 555,659 completed trades across 278 firms and three holding horizons.
### 6.3.13 Why the model architecture matters for the thesis contribution
The implemented architecture implies a deliberately limited causal interpretation. The conventional GARCH-family component captures information already contained in return dynamics. The ESG augmentation adds a slow-moving non-financial information set to the fitted conditional-variance state. If an ESG model changes the out-of-sample forecast, the empirical result shows that ESG information changes the model-based conditional expectation of variance; it does not establish that ESG is the structural cause of the future return shock.
Similarly, if the forecast-implied volatility gap changes, the result does not isolate whether the option market is inefficient, whether the ESG forecast captures a different physical-measure expectation, or whether other option-pricing forces are responsible. This is why the thesis does not interpret the gap as a complete arbitrage measure or as a formal decomposition of the theoretical variance risk premium.
The empirical sequencing is therefore deliberate. Forecast evaluation comes first because a trading signal based on an unvalidated forecast would conflate statistical model error with option-market mispricing. The option stage comes second and tests whether different forecasts imply materially different Black-76 values and volatility gaps. The trading stage comes last and asks whether those differences survive exact-contract implementation, delta neutralisation, longer-maturity vega hedging and fixed holding periods.
In summary, the 15 specifications should be understood as three volatility dynamics crossed with five information configurations: conventional GARCH, GJR-GARCH and EGARCH; composite ESG augmentation; and three pillar-specific ESG augmentations. The conventional alpha, beta and gamma parameters retain their standard GARCH-family meanings. The ESG coefficients are separate exogenous-information loadings estimated on the fitted log-variance process. This distinction is the key methodological clarification required for interpreting and reproducing the final empirical engine.
